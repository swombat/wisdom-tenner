<p>On a first reading, follow the book from beginning to end: the argument builds as it goes. When you return with a particular difficulty, these questions may help you find your place.</p>

<p><strong>“I know my subject. Why is it so difficult to make others understand it?”</strong></p>

<p>Start with <strong>Part I — Expertise is complex; it must become homely and clear</strong>. The knowledge you acquired and the work of making it accessible are not the same competence.</p>

<p><strong>“Why does a simple explanation persuade—even when it is wrong?”</strong></p>

<p>Read <strong>Part II — Why simplification works</strong>, then <strong>Part V — The dark side of ‘simple’</strong>. The features that help understanding can also create its illusion.</p>

<p><strong>“What can earlier thinkers teach me about this work?”</strong></p>

<p>Turn to <strong>Part III — A heritage of thought about understanding</strong>: different approaches to learning, interpretation, complexity and the limits of our minds.</p>

<p><strong>“Tomorrow I must explain, teach or advise. Where do I begin?”</strong></p>

<p>Go to <strong>Part IV — The practice of making simple</strong>, especially the tactical cycle. Begin with the people and their situation, not merely with the information you want to deliver.</p>

<p><strong>“Am I making the matter accessible—or making it falsely easy?”</strong></p>

<p>Return to <strong>Part V</strong>. Use the comparison between vulgarisation and making-simple to examine what your explanation preserves, what it loses, and what it enables the receiver to do.</p>

<p><strong>“Should I simplify this at all?”</strong></p>

<p>Read <strong>Part VI — The wisdom to simplify—or not</strong>. Making something easier to grasp is not, by itself, a sufficient reason to intervene.</p>

<p>These are starting places, not substitutes for the whole argument.</p>
